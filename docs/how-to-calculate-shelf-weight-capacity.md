# How to Calculate Shelf Weight Capacity: A Worked Example

Working out whether shelving can hold a load takes three numbers: the **shelf rating**, the **bay rating** and the **total weight planned for each shelf**.

## Step 1: Find the two ratings

Look on the product label or listing for:

- **Shelf rating (UDL):** the most one shelf holds with the weight spread evenly, e.g. 200kg
- **Bay rating:** the most the whole unit holds across all shelves, e.g. 800kg

These are separate limits. Four 200kg shelves do not guarantee an 800kg bay on every product.

## Step 2: Add up each shelf

Typical weights for full, everyday items:

| Item | Approx. weight |
|---|---|
| Paint tin 5L | 6.5 kg |
| Plastic storage box 64L, full | 18 kg |
| Archive box of paper | 15 kg |
| Metal toolbox, loaded | 15 kg |
| Car tyre on alloy wheel | 20 kg |
| Bag of cement | 25 kg |

## Step 3: Compare, with headroom

Worked example for a 2m garage bay rated 200kg per shelf and 800kg per bay:

| Shelf | Contents | Total | % of 200kg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top | 6 × 2.5L paint tins | 21 kg | 11% |
| 2 | 4 × 45L boxes | 48 kg | 24% |
| 3 | 2 toolboxes + 3 power tools | 42 kg | 21% |
| Bottom | 10 × 5L paint tins + 4 tyres | 101 kg | 51% |
| **Bay** | | **212 kg** | **27% of 800kg** |

Every shelf passes, but shelf 2 carries more than shelf 3 below it. Swapping them keeps the heaviest weight lower, which is safer.

A sensible target is **below about 75%** of each rating, which leaves room for boxes that turn out heavier than expected.

## Step 4: Adjust for concentrated loads

If most of a shelf's weight sits in one spot, plan to a lower figure. See [UDL vs point load](udl-vs-point-load.md).
